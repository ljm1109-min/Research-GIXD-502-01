Homelessness in Orange County

Photo: Kyle Grillot / The New York Times, 2024 · Skid Row, Los Angeles

What shapes the problem

Homelessness is not one story. It grows from many forces at once. Rent and income. A death, a divorce, an illness. A waitlist. A missing safety net.

I focus on Orange County as a case study: it is where I live, it has strong local data, and it shows both progress and the gaps that progress can hide.

Archeology

How the crisis was built

Decades of policy choices and economic shocks set up what we see today.

  1. 1967
    Lanterman-Petris-Short ActDeinstitutionalization begins. Psychiatric hospitals close, and community care never fully replaces them.
  2. 1978
    Proposition 13Limits local property-tax revenue, shaping how much housing and public service local governments can fund.
  3. 1980s
    Federal housing cuts and recessionsAffordable housing shrinks. Late baby boomers enter a weak job market and struggle to build savings.
  4. 2008
    Financial crisisJob losses and foreclosures arrive at the same time.
  5. 2018
    Martin v. BoiseCities can't punish sleeping outside when no shelter beds are available.
  6. 2019–
    $20B+ in state spendingA historic investment, yet California's crisis remains among the worst in the U.S.
  7. 2024
    Grants Pass v. JohnsonThe Supreme Court lets cities enforce camping bans again.
  8. 2025
    State SAFE Task ForceFaster encampment removals in large cities, including Anaheim.

California has 1 in 9 Americans, but 1 in 4 of those experiencing homelessness.

Source: U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development, 2024 Point-in-Time count (187,000+ in California of about 771,000 nationwide), via GV Wire / CalMatters, 2025

Archeology

Improving, except for its seniors

In 2018 the county cleared the Santa Ana River Trail encampment. Shelter plans for Irvine, Huntington Beach and Laguna Niguel were withdrawn after resident protests. Since then, the count has risen and fallen.

  • −26.6%Unsheltered homelessness, 2024 to 2026
  • 3,256 › 3,065For the first time, more people in shelters than on the streets
  • +1.5%Seniors 62 and older, the one group that grew · 882 people: 521 sheltered, 361 unsheltered
Paradox

Why hasn't it been solved?

  1. Funding

    Big in dollars, small in share

    California has spent billions since 2019, yet homelessness funding is still less than 1% of the state budget.

  2. Data

    The count decides funding, but misses people

    A RAND study found LA's count missed 26–32% of unsheltered people in some neighborhoods. People who are doubled up aren't counted at all.

  3. Enforcement

    Clearing camps moves people, not the problem

    Advocates say removals push people into parks and onto sidewalks, and make them harder to count.

  4. Community

    Needed, just not here

    In Orange County, shelter proposals have repeatedly been stopped by neighborhood opposition.

  5. Timing

    Help arrives after housing is lost

    Federal funds prioritize people already homeless. State prevention funding changes year to year.

  6. Framing

    Good news can hide bad news

    Orange County's overall decline in 2026 masked a rise among seniors.

The system is mostly designed to respond to homelessness, not to prevent it.

Misconceptions

What we get wrong

WrongCorrect

People come to California for the weather.

9 in 10 people experiencing homelessness in California lost their housing in California.

Homeless people are mostly young.

Nearly half (48%) of single homeless adults in California are 50 or older.

They've always been homeless.

41% of older homeless adults became homeless for the first time after 50. Many had been married and working full-time.

Most homeless people sleep on the street.

In Orange County's 2026 count, more people were in shelters than unsheltered, for the first time.

Nothing could have prevented it.

Many people said small amounts of money would have stopped their slide into homelessness.

If the count goes down, the problem is solved.

Counts miss people. A RAND study found LA's count missed 26–32% in some neighborhoods.

Homelessness is closer than we think, often just one life event away.

Emerging angles

Where to look next

Most promising

First-time senior homelessness

Orange County's total fell 13.7% in 2026, but seniors rose 1.5%. In California, 41% of older homeless adults became homeless for the first time after 50. Prevention programs such as Home Safe exist but are small and don't reach isolated seniors well.

Angle 2

Vehicle dwellers

A suburban form of homelessness. In LA, vehicle dwelling and rough sleeping rose 11% in 2025, and car dwellers in Anaheim have called for safe parking areas.

Angle 3

Hidden homelessness

People who are doubled up or newly homeless often don't appear in the count. One Orange County service center reported taking in more than twice as many newly homeless people as the count showed.
